THE BIG IDEA
A Lewis structure is a map of where a molecule's valence electrons sit: which are shared in bonds and which sit alone on an atom. There's one simple count that tells you how many bonds a molecule needs, and from there drawing the structure is bookkeeping. Almost everything later in the course (shapes, polarity, hybridization) starts from a correct Lewis structure.

KEY CONCEPTS

1. Lewis symbols (5.4)
An atom's Lewis symbol shows its valence electrons as dots on four sides, one per side before any pair up. For main-group elements, valence electrons = group number. N (5A) has 5 dots; Cl (7A) has 7.

2. The octet rule (5.5)
Nonmetals share electrons until each has 8 valence electrons around it, a filled s + p shell. Hydrogen is the exception: it wants only 2 (a "duet"), so hydrogen always has exactly one bond. Rare exceptions show up with odd electron counts or with boron.

3. Counting shared pairs: SP = ½(ER − VE)
This is the one formula to know from this reading.
- ER (electrons required) = 8 × (number of non-H atoms) + 2 × (number of H atoms). That's how many electrons you'd need if nobody shared.
- VE (valence electrons) = total valence electrons from all atoms. For an ion, add one for each negative charge and subtract one for each positive charge.
- SP = number of bonding pairs, i.e. how many lines you'll draw.
Sharing is what closes the gap between what's needed (ER) and what's available (VE).

4. Bond order, length and strength (5.5)
Bond order = number of shared pairs between two atoms: single (1), double (2), triple (3). Higher bond order means a shorter, stronger bond. Example from Table 5.5: C–C is 1.54 Å and 347 kJ/mol, C=C is 1.33 Å and 612 kJ/mol, and C≡C is 1.20 Å and 820 kJ/mol. Only compare bonds between the same two elements (C–O with C=O, not C–O with N–N).

5. The four-step method (5.6)
  1. Find SP = ½(ER − VE).
  2. Draw the skeleton: connect every atom with single bonds, then add the remaining shared pairs as double or triple bonds.
  3. Add lone pairs until every non-H atom has 8.
  4. Check: every atom has an octet (H has 2), and the total electrons drawn = VE.
Rule: never put a double or triple bond on hydrogen or on a halogen.

KEY TERMS
Valence electrons: outer-shell electrons; for main-group atoms, equal to the group number.
Lewis symbol: element symbol with its valence electrons as dots.
Octet rule: nonmetals end up with 8 valence electrons in a Lewis structure.
Duet: hydrogen's 2-electron closed shell.
Bonding (shared) pair: 2 electrons shared between atoms, drawn as a line.
Lone pair: 2 electrons on one atom and not in a bond, drawn as two dots.
Bond order: number of shared pairs in a bond (1, 2 or 3).

WORKED EXAMPLE 1: carbon monoxide, CO (Exercise 5.6)
- ER = 8 + 8 = 16 (two non-H atoms)
- VE = 4 (C) + 6 (O) = 10
- SP = ½(16 − 10) = 3, a triple bond
- Draw C≡O, then give each atom one lone pair so each has 8 (6 from the bond + 2).
- Check: 3 bonds × 2 = 6, plus 2 lone pairs × 2 = 4, total 10 = VE ✓. The C–O bond order is 3.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2: carbon dioxide, CO2 (Exercise 5.7)
- ER = 3 × 8 = 24
- VE = 4 + 6 + 6 = 16
- SP = ½(24 − 16) = 4
- Skeleton O–C–O uses 2 pairs; add the other 2 as a second bond on each side: O=C=O.
- Each O gets 2 lone pairs, and C already has 8 from its four bonds.
- Check: 4 bonds × 2 = 8, plus 4 lone pairs × 2 = 8, total 16 ✓. Each C–O bond order is 2.

QUICK SELF-CHECK: ammonia, NH3
ER = 8 + 3 × 2 = 14 · VE = 5 + 3 = 8 · SP = 3 · N has 1 lone pair.

NAMING MOLECULAR COMPOUNDS (the naming half of today's lecture is in 5.3, which isn't in the assigned pages)
Two nonmetals: use prefixes (mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, hexa-) and give the second element an "-ide" ending. Drop "mono-" on the first element. Examples: CO is carbon monoxide, N2O4 is dinitrogen tetroxide, PCl5 is phosphorus pentachloride. Ionic compounds (Ch 4) do NOT use prefixes, so don't mix up the two systems.

WHAT TO WATCH FOR IN LECTURE
- For ions, forgetting to adjust VE for the charge is the #1 error. CO3²⁻ has 4 + 18 + 2 = 24 valence electrons, not 22.
- In CO, carbon ends up with a triple bond and a lone pair, which looks strange. It's correct. Section 5.8 (Thursday) explains why using formal charge.
- 5.6 hints at resonance (CO3²⁻ can be drawn more than one way). That's Thursday's topic, 5.7.
